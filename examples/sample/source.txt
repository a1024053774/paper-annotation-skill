Does Marginal Annotation Improve Comprehension of Argumentative Texts?
A Classroom Study

A. Sample and B. Example

1. Introduction

Students are routinely told to annotate what they read, yet the advice rests on
surprisingly little evidence. Most studies of annotation measure recall of iso-
lated facts, not whether a reader can reconstruct the argument a text makes.

We use the term argument map for a reader’s account of which claims a text
defends and which reasons support each claim. An argument map is not a summary:
a summary may be accurate while leaving the structure of support entirely implicit.

We argue that marginal annotation improves comprehension only when it forces the
reader to label the function of each paragraph. Annotation that merely highlights
or paraphrases, we will show, adds little beyond rereading.

2. Method and Results

We assigned 126 second-year undergraduates at one university to three conditions:
functional annotation, free highlighting, and rereading. All participants read the
same 2,400-word philosophical essay and, two days later, drew an argument map
from memory, which two raters scored blind to condition.

    score = (correct claims + correct support links) / (total claims + total support links)

Table 1. Mean argument-map scores by condition (standard deviations in parentheses).

Functional annotators scored 0.71 on average, against 0.52 for highlighters and
0.49 for rereaders. The difference between highlighting and rereading was not statis-
tically significant, whereas functional annotation outperformed both (p < .01).

3. Objections and Conclusion

    Labeling paragraphs is slow, and slow reading is simply more reading; the
    benefit you observe may be time on task under another name.

This objection has force, and we cannot fully exclude it. However, functional
annotators spent on average only four minutes longer than rereaders, and within
the functional group time spent did not predict scores. It therefore seems unlikely
that time alone explains the effect, although a design that fixes reading time
would settle the matter.

Annotation, then, is not one practice but several, and only the kind that makes
structure explicit appears to help. Teachers who recommend annotation should say
which kind they mean.
